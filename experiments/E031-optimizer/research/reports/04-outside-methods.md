# Outside methods for shortening a found route

A research report made for E031's plan (2026-11-16) by a reading agent; its claims were used in the plan after the plan's critics checked them. Evidence paths are as the agent gave them.

## Findings

### 1. Measured on full5: most of the route's time sits in stops that win nothing, not in slow inputs. The path from boot to the end has 1,037 stops. 219 of them set a new bit or owner or raise the level, and they take 33,663 frames. The other 818 take 78,587 frames, 70% of the 112,250 frames after the first click. Those 818 are 428 single-target clicks (25,803 frames), 287 verb trials (30,641), 48 walks (11,674) and 55 waits (10,469). The route enters a room 464 times across 82 rooms, and one room alone is entered 37 times. A median of 33 frames separates two clicks.

- **Evidence:** A python pass over /var/tmp/fast-mi/direct/full5/leg00..leg03/nodes.jsonl that followed parents from the top stops 15923, 11189, 22320 and 7895 (script: /var/tmp/fast-mi/e031-research/path2.py), and a gap analysis of experiments/E027-direct/results/full5/end-stop.jsonl. The 70% is an upper bound on what can be removed: some of these stops change script variables that later steps need, and the 'news' field does not record those.
- **Why it matters:** The order of methods follows from this. Removing and reordering whole stops comes first, and frame-level TAS tightening comes last. For scale: full5 took 694,701 ticks (3:12:58), and the human Any% record of 21:52 is about 78,720 ticks, so the human route is about 8.8 times shorter.

### 2. The existing search tree has few shortcuts in it. Go-Explore's rule of keeping the earliest stop per cell, applied after the fact to nodes.jsonl, gains almost nothing. At each level-up on the path, the earliest stop anywhere in the same leg with the same level and the same set of objects ever owned comes only 0 to 157 frames earlier than the path's stop. The one larger gap, in leg03 at level 11, is the final stop: its frame includes the roughly 6,400-frame ending after the last click.

- **Evidence:** path.py in the same scratchpad, run over the 58,279 kept stops (leg sizes 16,005, 11,432, 22,943 and 7,899).
- **Why it matters:** Any Go-Explore-style or graph shortcut method has to expand new states around the path. Picking from the stops the search already kept is not enough.

### 3. Tool-assisted speedrun (TAS) practice: what an improvement loop uses. A movie is a list of inputs, each on an exact frame. A greenzone is a cache of savestates along the movie, so jumping to any frame costs only a short re-simulation. Bookmarks and branches keep alternative versions. Improvements are hand edits followed by a resync, and moving inputs can desync everything after the edit, including the game's random numbers. Automatic improvers exist for some games. TMInterface's bruteforce (Trackmania) and Radeline (Celeste) change the inputs of an existing movie at random or in sequence, re-simulate, and keep only improvements. Their users' main advice is to narrow the window rather than bruteforce a whole run. Luck manipulation means choosing input timing, often just waiting frames, so the random number generator gives a good result. Rare outcomes are brute-forced with scripts.

- **Evidence:** FCEUX TAS Editor glossary: Greenzone is 'The storage designed for speeding up Playback cursor navigation'; a Branch is 'A full copy of the movie' (https://fceux.com/web/help/taseditor/Glossary.html). TASVideos LuckManipulation: 'the act of controlling random decisions through input' and 'delaying button presses will be necessary, but is also sufficient' (https://tasvideos.org/LuckManipulation). Input editing and desync risk: https://tasvideos.org/InputFileEditing, https://tasvideos.org/Forum/Posts/11975. Bruteforce guidance: https://sai-moen.github.io/TMInterface-AS-SaiMoen/blog/bf_intuition. Radeline: https://github.com/Kataiser/radeline. The ScummVM TAS route is libTAS, with a movie that starts with no save data: https://tasvideos.org/EmulatorResources/ScummVM. Cook et al. 2025 state that 'TAS runs are not guided by AI search – they are made manually'.
- **Why it matters:** The bridge already has the parts a greenzone needs. fork() copies the whole process exactly, including the random state, and each Status reports 'rng' (guide 02-bridge-core.md, lines 61, 408 and 452-458). A full replay from boot takes 2.89 s (full5/verify-end.json). Forks at stop boundaries can therefore act as the greenzone, and each test re-simulates only a short window. This TAS layer is also where glitches enter: perturbing click points and frames is how walkbox and pixel exploits get found without writing a move by hand.

### 4. Ticks, not frames, must be the shortener's cost. The Any% clock runs in ticks. The logo speed glitch leaves var 19 at 5 instead of 6, so frame-paced motion takes the same number of frames but fewer ticks, while delay() and talk timers keep their tick length and so take more frames. full5 averages 6.04 ticks per frame (694,701 ticks over 114,978 frames), consistent with the timer staying at 6.

- **Evidence:** docs/related-work.md section 1.2 (the mechanism of the logo glitch); rules/anypercent.md (timing in ticks at 1/60 s on DOS CD; allowed: walkbox exploits, the logo glitch, item slides, saves made during the run, a chosen seed).
- **Why it matters:** If the shortener scores candidates in frames, it ranks the glitch wrong and can never find it. Scored in ticks, a generic search over which key to press at which boot frame, using keys taken from the input scripts, can find the logo glitch on its own. That keeps 'no move written by hand' and covers 'including glitches'.

### 5. Go-Explore and robustification. The archive keeps one state and trajectory per cell. A new trajectory replaces the stored one if it scores higher, or scores the same and is shorter. Later phases build on the stored states, but a better way into a cell is not propagated into trajectories already extended from it. Robustification uses Salimans and Chen's backward algorithm: start training near the end of a demonstration and move the start point back once about 20% of rollouts succeed. Results: the robustified policies on Montezuma's Revenge averaged 1,731,645 points and often beat the exploration-phase trajectories. The paper's warning is that a shorter trajectory found by restoring simulator state can be unreliable in a stochastic environment.

- **Evidence:** Ecoffet et al., Nature 2021 / arXiv 2004.12919 ('if a state maps to an already known cell, but is associated with a better trajectory (e.g. higher performing or shorter) ... will replace'; the highway example of shorter but unreliable trajectories), https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.12919. The 2019 version's rule (higher score, or equal score and shorter): https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.10995. Salimans and Chen 2018 (the reset point moves back once 20% succeed; the 71,500-point demonstration was itself made with a rewind tool): https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.03381.
- **Why it matters:** Here the engine is deterministic and the seed fixed, so robustification is not needed and the reliability warning does not apply. Two parts transfer. One is the cell rule with ticks as length. The other is its known gap: the better prefix is not propagated, so after a shortcut the suffix must be re-realised and replayed. Measured above, the rule adds little on the existing tree (0 to 157 frames), so it only pays off inside a search that expands new states (see the neighbourhood-search finding).

### 6. Delta debugging (ddmin) and validity-preserving reduction. ddmin removes chunks of a failing input, first halves then finer pieces, until removing any single element breaks the test: a 1-minimal result. Its guarantees assume the outcome changes monotonically as parts are removed. A game route breaks that assumption, because removing a stop changes positions, timing and the random state downstream. In practice it has reduced OpenGL call traces to under 0.1% of their size and shortened replayed web-event recordings, and SimplyDroid applied a hierarchical version over a GUI state tree to 92 Android crash traces. Removing raw events tends to produce invalid inputs. Hypothesis avoids this by reducing the generator's own choice sequence, and GReduce (ASE 2025) by reducing the generator's execution trace, so every candidate is one the generator could have produced.

- **Evidence:** Zeller and Hildebrandt, TSE 2002: https://www.st.cs.uni-saarland.de/papers/tse2002/tse2002.pdf. OpenGL traces: https://2022.esec-fse.org/details/a-test-2022-papers/9/OpenGL-API-Call-Trace-Reduction-with-the-Minimizing-Delta-Debugging-Algorithm. Web recordings: Bae et al., ESEC/FSE 2015 (DOI 10.1145/2786805.2786846). SimplyDroid, ASE 2017: https://bibtex.github.io/ASE-2017-JiangWLC.html. MacIver and Donaldson, ECOOP 2020: https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~afd/papers/2020/ECOOP_Hypothesis.pdf. GReduce: https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04623.
- **Why it matters:** Mapped onto this system: the generator's choice sequence is the list of the stops' 'how' names (v9t265, go12, wait200 and so on). Deleting raw clicks with absolute frames would desync everything after them. Instead the shortener deletes stops or chunks of stops and re-realises the rest with the search's own click realiser, each at the earliest frame it counts. The engine then judges each candidate by whether the next milestone is still reached; at the very end, whether bit 565 is set. Stops on the path are natural chunk boundaries, much as the GUI state tree was for SimplyDroid. Non-monotonicity only means some candidates fail, and replay catches them.

### 7. Plan post-optimisation from automated planning, the closest match to shortening a found route. Action Elimination (AE) works greedily: try removing one action together with every later action that loses a precondition, keep the shorter plan if it still reaches the goal, and repeat. The cost is O(n squared times p) checks. Finding the optimal reduction is NP-hard, and it has been solved exactly with weighted MaxSAT (Balyo et al. 2014) and as a planning task (Salerno et al. 2023). Plan Neighborhood Graph Search (PNGS) runs a bounded search of limit L from every state on the plan, takes the cheapest path through the union of those neighbourhoods with Dijkstra, and doubles L each round. In the Aras postprocessor, the two together improved the best published plans on 60 of 270 IPC-2008 instances. They would have lifted three planners from places 3 to 5 to second. They did best on 'several loosely coupled subtasks'. AE runs in under a second; PNGS takes 10 to 12 rounds of about 30 s each. The authors note that AE catches redundant supporting actions that PNGS misses, so the two complement each other.

- **Evidence:** Nakhost and Müller, ICAPS 2010 (DOI 10.1609/icaps.v20i1.13402), https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICAPS/article/view/13402 (text extracted from the PDF: Algorithm 1; 'most effective on problems consisting of several loosely coupled subtasks'; '60 instances'). Balyo, Chrpa and Kilani, SoCS 2014: https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/SOCS/article/view/18318. Salerno, Fuentetaja and Seipp, KR 2023: https://proceedings.kr.org/2023/80/kr2023-0080-salerno-et-al.pdf.
- **Why it matters:** An adventure game's errands are loosely coupled subtasks of exactly this kind. AE is the ddmin step with a planner's rule for which later actions to drop as well. PNGS is the expansion that Go-Explore's cell rule needs, with ticks as cost and the search's own move generator as M. Neither needs a model or an LLM, and neither changes the search's code: a separate binary can read nodes.jsonl and drive the same engine and forks.

### 8. Path shortcutting in motion planning and solution optimisers in Sokoban: the same idea from two other fields. Planners like RRT find jagged paths, and the standard post-process picks two points on the path and tries to join them directly. Geraerts and Overmars note that this gives no guarantee of optimality. Sokoban optimisers such as JSoko's 'vicinity' search start from an existing solution, generate nearby box configurations around every state on it, and run an ordinary shortest-path search in that set. A larger vicinity gives shorter solutions at the cost of more time and memory.

- **Evidence:** Geraerts and Overmars, IJRR 2007 (26(8):845-863), summarised at https://bengravell.github.io/knowledge-base/papers/2007_geraerts_creating_high_quality_paths/. Hauser and Ng-Thow-Hing, ICRA 2010 (time-parameterised shortcuts). JSoko: https://jsokoapplet.sourceforge.io/help/help/help/how-the-optimizer-works.html.
- **Why it matters:** These confirm that the PNGS design is the standard answer whenever an exploration search finds a feasible but jagged path. The shortcut test fits this system directly: from the fork at stop i, a bounded best-first search by ticks aims at stop j's milestone set, for j > i. The result is a local improvement only, so a claim about it must be measured in the engine and must not say 'optimal'.

### 9. Beam and best-first search with time as cost. Baumgarten's A* Mario agent used the game's forward model with time-to-goal as cost and won the 2009 Mario AI Competition, deciding within a 40 ms budget per step. In the AIIDE 2025 workshop paper, Cook et al. generated TAS files at random for PICO-8 games, with inputs every fifth frame. This rediscovered the spike-jump technique, and a greedy novelty search using the TAS tool's frame advance and rewind also ran. They suggest searching for glitches by making time part of the objective: only TAS files that finish faster than the known trick count.

- **Evidence:** Togelius, Karakovskiy and Baumgarten, CEC 2010: https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gp-html/Togelius_2010_cec.html. Cook, Charity, Awiszus, Carnovalini and Dockhorn, 'We Call This Controller Skip: AI for Speedrunning', CEUR Vol-4090: https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4090/paper1.pdf (text extracted: section 4.2 on glitch replication with Celia, 4.3, and 5.2 on routing).
- **Why it matters:** The direct search selects by progress (FMI_SELECT=progress, furthest), which is why it found the end but not a fast route. A second mode or program that ranks by ticks must run in a corridor: between consecutive milestones of the found route, with a per-cell beam. Run unbounded over the whole game, it would be the expensive global search the found route already makes unnecessary.

### 10. Speedrun routing research says little that is directly usable, but it frames the problem. Groß et al. argue that A* or Dijkstra on an event graph fails because edge weights 'change with the already traversed nodes'. Making the weights static needs a detailed state graph with vastly more nodes. They propose evolutionary algorithms and cite a genetic algorithm for TrackMania checkpoints and an evolutionary algorithm for Morrowind quests. Lafond proves speedrun routing NP-hard in general and gives an FPT result for bounded treewidth and in-degree. Cook et al. describe routing as both choosing tasks and ordering them, and as 'a lot of unexplored space'.

- **Evidence:** Groß, Zühlke and Naujoks, arXiv 2106.01182: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.01182. Lafond, FUN 2018: https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.FUN.2018.27. Cook et al. 2025, section 5.2. docs/optimization.md shows the project's A* with LM-cut over position-keyed measured costs, with the final pick made by measurement.
- **Why it matters:** The system answers Groß's objection by never trusting a cost table. Every candidate is replayed in the engine, so the engine is the cost model. Reordering milestones globally with A* and LM-cut over a learned milestone model would need that model built first, from the tree's milestone preconditions and the script reader. Against the local methods above, it costs more machinery for a gain not yet shown, so it fits as a later step. Lafond's hardness result supports local, measured improvement over claims of optimality.

### 11. Recommendation, in order. (1) Validity-preserving Action Elimination with ddmin-style chunking over the path's stops. Candidates are re-realised from the 'how' names at the earliest frame each counts, judged by engine replay from the nearest fork against the next milestone and finally bit 565, and scored in ticks. This is the cheapest step with the largest pool, the 818 stops that win nothing (78,587 frames), and it needs no model. (2) PNGS with Go-Explore's best-per-cell rule. Run a bounded best-first search by ticks from each state on the reduced path, using the search's own move generator. Merge states into cells keyed by milestone state and room, keep the earliest-tick trajectory per cell, take the cheapest path with Dijkstra, double the bound each round, and replay to verify. This catches reordering and cheaper ways to the same milestone, which deletion cannot. (3) TAS tightening with glitches, last, in narrow windows. Search for the earliest frame of each input, perturb click points and frames at random (TMInterface or Radeline style), search which key to press at which boot frame (this finds the logo glitch once cost is in ticks), and insert idle frames before stops that consume random numbers ('rng' is in every Status). Seed choice is allowed by rules/anypercent.md.

- **Evidence:** The measurements above. AE and PNGS results (60 of 270 IPC instances improved; strongest on loosely coupled subtasks). The validity-preserving reducers (Hypothesis, GReduce). The bruteforce guidance from the TAS community (narrow windows). A replay cost of 2.89 s from boot, and less from forks.
- **Why it matters:** All three run as a separate program reading nodes.jsonl and end-stop.jsonl, so the search's results stay byte-identical and gate.sh still passes. Each result is a new movie checked by 'direct verify'-style replay from boot. The order puts structural waste (most of the 8.8 times gap) before frame-level gains, as the TAS bruteforce community advises.

## Open points

- Can a separate binary call the search's click realiser (from 'how' names to clicks at the earliest frame they count) and its move generator as a library, leaving the search's code and outputs untouched? Or would that need a refactor that the gate must re-check?
- Which state key makes two stops interchangeable for the Go-Explore and PNGS merge (bits, owners, room, ego position, script variables, rng)? Too coarse a key wastes replays; too fine a key finds no shortcuts. Only replay decides in the end.
- Does the bridge report ticks per stop, so the shortener can score in ticks? The movie and nodes.jsonl store frames only, and the logo glitch is invisible in frames.
- How many of the 818 stops that win nothing change script variables a later step needs (dialogue state, counters)? The 78,587-frame pool is an upper bound until AE runs.
- Which route stops consume random numbers (rng changes between forks), and how much could idle-frame luck manipulation or a different seed save? A different seed probably forces re-realising the whole route.
- Are the 2,728 frames before the first click, and the roughly 6,400-frame ending, fixed costs under the Any% timing (start at the logo skip, end at the final fade), or do they hold time a key search could save?
- Stop rule for the experiment: what improvement in ticks after AE alone would justify building PNGS, and what would justify the TAS glitch layer?
